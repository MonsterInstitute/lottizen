It's the first question many winners ask and the one they least want the answer to: can I keep this quiet? In Canada, the honest answer is **almost never**. Every one of the five regional lottery corporations makes publicity a *condition* of being paid a major prize — not a courtesy you can decline. Anonymity exists only as a rare, discretionary exception, and the legal structures people assume will hide them mostly don't. Here's how it actually works, province by province.

## Why every province makes winners go public

The lottery corporations are government or Crown entities, and their core defence against the suspicion that games are rigged is simple: prove that real, named people actually win. Publishing winners — name, town, photo — is that proof. As one OLG spokesperson framed it, winners want privacy, "but we need to balance those concerns with our requirement as a government agency to be transparent about our winners."

So publicity isn't administrative box-ticking; it's the integrity mechanism of the whole system. That's why it's written into the rules as a payment condition, and why "I'd rather not" isn't an option above the threshold.

## The rules, province by province

| Corporation | Region | Anonymous? | Publicity threshold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| OLG | Ontario | **No** | Name + city at $1,000+; photo at $10,000+ |
| BCLC | British Columbia | **Rare** (safety only) | Consent mandatory; process at $25,000+ |
| WCLC | MB / SK / AB + territories | **No** (rare safety only) | Photo + interview at $10,000+ |
| Loto-Québec | Quebec | **No** | **No threshold** — all prize levels |
| ALC | Atlantic Canada | **No** | $25,000+ |

**OLG (Ontario).** Publicity is a contractual condition. Name and city are posted on OLG.ca for 30 days for any prize of $1,000 or more; a published photo is required at $10,000 or more. Winners sign a declaration form releasing OLG to publish their name, town, prize, game, and photo. This governs the Ontario-only games — [Ontario 49](/canada/ontario-49), [Lottario](/canada/lottario), and [MegaDice](/canada/megadice) — as well as national games claimed in Ontario.

**BCLC (British Columbia).** Written consent to publish is mandatory, and BCLC states that if a claimant refuses consent, "a prize may be withheld." Larger prizes get *more* publicity, not less — the full process (in-person claim, photo, interview) kicks in at $25,000.

**WCLC (Western Canada).** The rule is explicit: "the Corporation has no obligation to pay a prize unless the winner gives WCLC the right to publish the winner's name, city or town of residence and recent photograph." An interview and photo are required for all claims of $10,000 or more. Alberta's AGLC operates through WCLC on the same terms.

**ALC (Atlantic Canada).** For prizes of $25,000 or more, "you are required to let us use your full name, city, province, prize amount, game won on, and a current photograph." ALC may publicize for up to a year; the only thing you can decline is publicity *beyond* that first year.

## The Quebec myth

There's a persistent belief that Quebec winners can stay private. It's wrong — and if anything, backwards. Loto-Québec's binding regulations (section 13(b)) state the Corporation has no obligation to pay unless the holder provides "all consents in writing… granting… the right to publish the name, address, place of residence and a recent photograph." Crucially, there is **no dollar threshold** — the consent requirement applies at every prize level. That arguably makes Quebec *stricter* than Ontario or BC, not more lenient. The "first names only" you see on Loto-Québec's social media and cheques is a presentation choice, not anonymity; full names still appear in its releases and on its site.

## The one real exception: public safety

Anonymity is granted, but rarely, and only for a genuine safety reason. BCLC is the most explicit: it considers requests "on a case by case basis, but the exceptions are rare," reserved for "a real public safety concern… substantiated with evidence that is verifiable and capable of independent confirmation." Think abuse survivors, extortion targets, undercover officers, or informants — not simply a preference for privacy.

The scale tells the story. When a BC winner of **$30 million** was granted anonymity in 2018, it was reported as only the *fourth* such approval in three years, and the only one for a prize over $100,000. WCLC applies a similar informal standard for safety-sensitive roles. If your reason is "I don't want the attention," that does not clear the bar.

## What doesn't work — and what a lawyer can do

The two structures people reach for both fail:

- **Trusts, numbered companies, LLCs.** Canadian corporations require the prize be claimed by an **individual** (or a group of individuals), never an entity. There is no US-style provision to name a trust as payee anywhere in Canada. A BC winner of **$50 million** learned this the hard way: he tried to have a trust claim the ticket, BCLC refused, and after roughly 21 months and about $500,000 in legal costs he transferred it back to himself and collected — with full publicity.

- **A lawyer as intermediary** helps at the margins. A lawyer can take lawful possession of the ticket and handle the claim on your behalf, which reduces your *practical* exposure. But it does not override the corporation's right to publicize the actual winner. It buys distance, not anonymity.

## 2026: a small step toward privacy

The one recent softening is modest. As of January 2026, BCLC publishes only a first name and last initial (plus photo and story); OLG followed in about May 2026 by dropping last names from its *news releases* — both citing scams that target big winners. But this is not anonymity: OLG still posts full names on OLG.ca for 30 days, and the photo requirement is unchanged. WCLC, as of writing, hasn't adopted the change at all. The direction is winner-protective, but the substance of publicity remains.

## What this means before you claim

If privacy is important to you, plan around the reality rather than hoping to escape it:

- **Assume you'll be named.** Above a low threshold in every province, your name and town become public, and usually a photo. Decide in advance how you'll handle the attention — and consider getting your affairs (and advisors) in order *before* the announcement, not after.
- **Don't count on a trust or a "first names only" province.** Neither works the way the internet suggests.
- **A safety exemption is real but narrow.** If you genuinely have a documented safety concern, raise it early, with evidence — but treat it as the rare exception it is.

Anonymity is the part of winning most people get wrong before they win. Knowing the rule in advance is the only real protection it offers.
