Every Canadian lottery ticket carries a quiet legal fact that most players never think about until something goes wrong: it is a **bearer instrument**. The prize belongs to whoever legitimately holds and presents the ticket — not, by default, to whoever paid for it. Loto-Québec states it plainly: when a winning ticket is presented, "the prize is always paid to the legitimate holder of the ticket."

That single principle explains everything about lost, stolen, and damaged tickets — including why the most valuable thing you can do takes five seconds and a pen. Here is what actually happens in each case, and what your options really are.

## Sign it first — this is the whole game

Before anything can go wrong, sign the back of your ticket. This isn't a friendly suggestion; it's the mechanism that ties a bearer instrument to *you*. Across every corporation the rule is the same: a retailer **cannot validate an unsigned ticket**. OLG puts it directly — "all players must first sign their tickets… This ensures that only the correct owner can claim the ticket."

The moment you've signed it, take one more step the corporations now actively recommend: **photograph the front and back**. Loto-Québec advises players to "take photos of the front and back of your ticket." BCLC frames it as evidence — if the ticket is later lost, "you will have evidence to support your claim for an investigation if you take clear photos of the ticket after your purchase." Thirty seconds of preparation is the difference between a strong claim and a hopeless one. Whether it's a [Lotto 6/49](/canada/lotto-6-49), [Lotto Max](/canada/lotto-max), or [Ontario 49](/canada/ontario-49) ticket, the routine is identical.

## If you lost your ticket

The hard truth first: the general rule is **no ticket, no prize**. The ticket — specifically its control number — is the proof of the claim. WCLC is explicit that "no prize will be paid if the ticket is not confirmed as a winner through its Control Number," and that it "will require the original ticket, together with identification, at the time of prize claim."

There is one meaningful exception worth knowing, and OLG is the most accommodating corporation about it:

> "If you believe you had a winning ticket but lost it, OLG may still be able to verify your claim using data analysis."

To pursue that, you call OLG Customer Care and be ready to say **where and when** you bought the ticket. BCLC takes a similar investigative approach, but leans on the evidence you kept — those photos again. None of this is a guarantee. A discretionary investigation is not the same as a right to be paid, and a signature on a ticket you can no longer produce does not, by itself, let you claim. The signed-and-photographed ticket is what gives an investigation something to work with.

## If your ticket was stolen

Here the signature does its real work. Because a validated claim requires a signature, an unsigned stolen ticket can be cashed by the thief — while a signed one is far harder for anyone else to redeem. That is precisely why BCLC tells players to sign immediately, "so… if the ticket is misplaced or you accidentally lose the ticket and someone else finds it, they won't be able to claim the winnings."

If a ticket you believe is a winner is stolen, contact the corporation's customer or player care line right away so the ticket can be flagged. High-value claims are investigated as a matter of course regardless — BCLC reviews all claims of $25,000 or more (and retailer claims over $2,000) through its Corporate Security team, "who also interview the claimants," and can review any claim where there are "concerns about the ownership of a winning ticket." Reporting a theft to the police is prudent practice, though it's handled case by case rather than as a published rule.

## If your ticket is damaged

Good news lives here. A torn, washed, faded, or misprinted ticket is **not automatically void**, because the prize is determined by the encoded control number in the corporation's central system — not by the readable face. Loto-Québec's rules capture both halves of the principle: a mutilated, illegible, or defective ticket is null, "except if Loto-Québec can determine by means of the ticket's control number that the ticket is actually winning the claimed prize."

So the deciding question is simply: **can the control number still be read and matched?** If yes, a damaged ticket can usually still be paid. The process is to take it — or mail it — to the corporation's customer-care office, not a store terminal. ALC's guidance is representative: "If your ticket seems damaged, you should call Atlantic Lottery's Customer Care Department." OLG similarly may ask you to send the original ticket in for processing and data analysis. What you should *not* do is keep running a failing ticket through a retail scanner; a ticket that fails the corporation's validation tests is void, and that determination is final.

## The one-year clock

Damaged or pristine, every ticket is on a deadline, and it's remarkably consistent across the country: **one year from the draw date** for terminal (draw) tickets. OLG — "exactly one year from the draw date." WCLC, ALC, BCLC and Loto-Québec all state the same 52-week / one-year window. Scratch tickets are different: they expire on the date printed on the back.

The deadline is firm. BCLC notes that if a player finds a missing winning ticket "after 52 weeks are up, they will no longer be able to claim their prize." An expired ticket is simply an expired ticket — which is another argument for checking tickets promptly rather than letting a pile accumulate in a drawer.

## How to protect yourself

Everything above collapses into a short, boring, effective routine — do these four things at the counter and the failure modes mostly disappear:

- **Sign the back immediately**, in ink, before you leave the store. An unsigned ticket protects no one but a thief.
- **Photograph the front and back.** This is now official corporation advice, not just prudence, and it's what turns a lost-ticket dead end into an investigable claim.
- **Store it flat, dry, and somewhere you'll remember** — the control number is what gets validated, so keep it readable.
- **Check it within the year.** The one-year clock doesn't pause for anyone.

None of this improves your odds of winning — that's fixed the moment the numbers are drawn. But it's the entire difference between holding a prize and holding a story about one.
