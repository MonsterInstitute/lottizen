Pooling money for lottery tickets with coworkers is one of the most common ways Canadians play — and one of the most reliable ways a jackpot turns into a lawsuit. The stories are a genre unto themselves: the colleague who missed a payment, the friend who cashed the ticket, the group that "always played together" until it didn't. Almost every one of these fights was preventable with a single sheet of paper. Here's how group claims actually work, why the split isn't taxed, and what the courts have taught us about avoiding the courtroom.

## How a group actually claims a prize

Every Canadian corporation has a formal group-claim process, built around a designated leader and a form that names each member's share. The mechanics differ:

- **WCLC, ALC, and BCLC use a trustee model.** The corporation pays the **entire prize to one designated trustee**, who then distributes each member's share. WCLC has the trustee print their name plus "**In Trust**" on the back of the ticket to mark it as group-owned; its free **Group Buying Agreement** records the trustee, the games and draws, and every member's share (kept by the group, but WCLC will ask for it on a win over $1,000). BCLC requires a **Group Prize Agreement** for wins above roughly $2,000, with each member declaring their share. ALC requires a **Group Win Agreement** — a Commissioner of Oaths signature for wins of $1,000–$24,999, and all members present for anything above $25,000.

- **OLG names every member.** Rather than paying a single trustee, OLG uses a **Lottery Prize Claim Group List** on which each of the two-or-more claimants is named and declares a share; the Group Leader submits the claim and OLG collects each member's information. Its online Group Play splits small wins automatically to members' accounts.

The common thread: above a low threshold, the split has to be **documented on the corporation's own form** at claim time. The disputes below are almost all about groups that never did the equivalent *before* the win.

## The tax angle — split it right

Good news first: a shared prize is **not taxed** when it's split. Canadian lottery winnings are a windfall, not income, so the CRA doesn't tax the prize, and Canada has no gift tax — so distributing shares isn't a taxable event either. (Only the *income* the money later earns is taxable; that's covered in [Are Lottery Winnings Taxable in Canada?](/guides/are-lottery-winnings-taxable-in-canada).)

There's a subtle reason to document the split **before** the draw, though. If each member's share is established only *after* one person has already received the whole prize, it can start to look like a *gift* from that winner rather than each member's own windfall — which can matter for later attribution and estate questions. When the group is named on the corporation's form, or a written agreement predates the purchase, every member's share is cleanly their own tax-free windfall. It's prudent practice rather than a CRA rule, but it costs nothing and removes all doubt.

## What the courts have decided

Canadian judges have heard these disputes many times, and a consistent principle runs through them: **ownership follows contribution and agreement — not who cashed the ticket, and not who feels deserving.**

- **Spiro v. Koc (2016 ONCA 592).** Bank employees ran a pool; one member contributed to a ticket bought June 25, then left the job weeks later. A new hire who joined afterward cashed the tickets and discovered that June 25 ticket had won $1,000,000. The Ontario Court of Appeal held the **contributors to that specific ticket** were its co-owners — the person who cashed it, having not contributed to it, was owed nothing. Ownership attached to the ticket, on the day it was bought.

- **The Bombardier $50-million pool (settled 2014).** A [Lotto Max](/canada/lotto-max) pool at an Ontario plant won $50 million while one founding member was on vacation and had missed his $5 that week. The group invoked "no pay, no play." OLG paid most of the prize but held **$2 million in trust** pending trial; the parties settled on day two. The lesson isn't that "no pay, no play" is invalid — it's enforceable in principle — but that the rule for **absent members** hadn't been clearly agreed in writing, which is what made it litigable.

- **Maan (BC, 2025).** Warehouse workers said they pooled money twice weekly; one member won $2 million on a [BC/49](/canada/bc-49) ticket and didn't disclose it until BCLC posted his cheque. The BC Supreme Court found **no evidence the winning ticket was bought with pooled money** — the purchase pattern didn't match the claimed contributions. The plaintiffs "may feel that they have a moral entitlement… [but] failed to establish legal entitlement." Playing together often was not enough.

- **Tsotsos (Windsor, settled 2025).** An *excluded* worker sued 16 coworkers after a $1-million [Lotto Max](/canada/lotto-max) Maximillion win, arguing the pool ran on a credit system he always settled; the group said he "did not pay to play." Four years of litigation ended in a confidential settlement — the entirely avoidable cost of an informal IOU arrangement no one had written down.

The pattern is unmistakable: courts look at documentation, the consistency of contributions, timing, and evidence of a specific agreement. General "we play together" history carries almost no weight.

## The mixed-ticket problem

Several of those cases turn on the same ambiguity: a pool member who *also* buys personal tickets. Which win is whose? The corporations try to pre-empt this by having the trustee **physically segregate and label** group tickets ("In Trust"); anything unmarked is presumed personal. In court, the winner claiming a ticket was personal must show it wasn't group property, and the pool must prove a binding agreement covering *that specific ticket*.

The practical defence is simple: buy personal tickets in a **separate transaction**, keep them physically apart, and make sure only the **labelled, photographed group tickets** are in the pool — with the agreement stating in writing that everything else is personal.

## A pool agreement that prevents fights

None of this requires a lawyer. A signed page — or even a group-chat message everyone acknowledges — that covers the following will pre-empt essentially every dispute above:

- **Every member's name** and the **contribution per person, per draw**.
- **Which draws, games, and dates** the pool covers, and the **specific tickets** in it each time.
- **How numbers are chosen** (Quick Pick or fixed).
- A clear **"no pay, no play" cut-off** — and, explicitly, **what happens to absent members** (auto-covered or excluded?). This is the single most litigated point; decide it in writing before it matters.
- **Who buys and holds the tickets**, holding contributions in trust.
- **Circulate a photo of every group ticket to all members before the draw**, so it's unambiguous which tickets are in.
- **How winnings are split** and who will act as trustee for the claim.

Each corporation offers a free form to build on — OLG's Group List, WCLC's Group Buying Agreement, BCLC's Group Prize Agreement, ALC's Group Win Agreement — and Ontario's AGCO even runs a lottery-ticket-disputes process for when it goes wrong. But the cheapest insurance is the boring one: agree the rules, in writing, before the numbers are drawn. Every case above is a story about the sheet of paper nobody made.
